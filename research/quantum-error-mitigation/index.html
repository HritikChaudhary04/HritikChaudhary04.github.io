Neural-network error mitigation for quantum teleportation and superdense coding

Under review 2026 With Gokul K.C., Kathmandu University
quantum informationneural networks error mitigationsimulation
Code (GitHub)

The problem

Quantum teleportation and superdense coding are two of the basic protocols of quantum communication. On real hardware both are hit by noise, and every result is estimated from a finite number of measurement shots. A common idea is to add a small neural network after measurement to clean up the output. I wanted to know how much such a network actually helps.

Why the comparison needs strong baselines

A network that beats the raw, uncorrected output is easy to build, so that comparison says little. The fair question is whether it beats the classical methods someone would use anyway: a decoder that knows the channel, or a direct fit of the calibration error. If it only matches them, the network has learned the classical answer, not found a better one.

My approach

  1. Superdense coding. The noise is a biased Pauli channel plus an asymmetric readout error. I compared majority vote, a channel-aware maximum-likelihood decoder, and a learned decoder, across different numbers of shots.
  2. Teleportation. The noise is a fixed systematic rotation $R_{\text{fix}}$ plus per-qubit jitter, and the output state is estimated by finite-shot tomography. I compared the uncorrected state, a least-squares estimate of the fixed rotation, and a learned corrector.
  3. Statistics. Every comparison is repeated over several random seeds and reported with 95% confidence intervals. The seed lists are fixed so the tables can be reproduced exactly.
  4. Generalisation test. I trained the corrector on one fixed rotation and tested it on a different one.

Results

  • In superdense coding the learned decoder beats majority vote but only matches the maximum-likelihood decoder; the two agree within the 95% intervals. The network approximates the optimal decoder; it does not exceed it.
  • In teleportation the least-squares baseline recovers most of the lost fidelity and estimates $R_{\text{fix}}^{-1}$ to a Frobenius distance of about 0.02. The network adds only about 1 to 2 points on top, from the nonlinear jitter and tomographic noise.
  • A corrector trained on one rotation does not transfer to another. It learns the specific calibration, not a general correction.

Where it stands
